Notes from my own research, plus what I’m reading about robotics in different countries and how I’d think about it as an embedded engineer. News items cite their sources; the opinions are mine.

28 Nov 2026ResearchWho checks the simulator?2 MIN

Almost everything I build starts in simulation: the FOC joint, the fault detector, the pick-and-place arm. I treat the simulator as ground truth, and so does most of robot learning, where policies are trained for millions of steps in an engine before they ever see hardware. That made me ask an uncomfortable question: how do I know the engine is simulating the robot I described, and nothing else?

It turns out you can check. If you have an independent model of the same robot, you can compare what the engine did, step by step, against what the physics says it should have done. In free motion the two agree to rounding error, so anything left over is physics that someone added: a damping term you didn’t set, a different inertia, a default motor still holding the joint. Contact is where engines differ most, and where it matters most for anything that grasps or walks.

That work is now a paper under peer review, so I am keeping the details for later. The habit it gave me is already useful: before I trust a simulated result, I check that the simulator is doing only what I asked.

27 Nov 2026ResearchHow do you test a fault detector before the hardware exists?2 MIN

My joint fault-isolation paper makes claims like ‘no false alarms’ and ‘faster than a following-error check’. With the bench not up yet, the only honest way to back them was a simulation study designed to try to break the detector, not to show it off.

Three rules shaped it. First, never give the detector the true model: every run drew fresh random errors in the model the detector uses: up to 20% in winding resistance, 10% in inertia and inductance, and 30% in friction. Second, calibrate thresholds once, on a few minutes of normal motion, and then test on runs the calibration never saw: 300 Monte Carlo fault trials and a separate hour of normal motion. Third, hold the baseline to the same rules, so the comparison is fair. Under those rules the detector isolated 299 of 300 faults with no false alarms, while the following-error check, calibrated the same way, alarmed in 22% of normal runs.

The study also found the limits, which I think matter more. A frozen encoder is invisible to the voltage residual below about 0.5 rad/s at the joint, and that low-speed case caused the one mislabelled fault. The plant also ignores cogging, backlash and compliance. Those are now the first things I will measure on hardware.

26 Nov 2026ResearchThe momentum observer, explained without the math2 MIN

A robot joint knows two things very well: the torque it is commanding and how fast it is moving. If you also know its inertia and friction, you can predict how its momentum should change. A momentum observer just keeps that prediction running and watches the gap between predicted and measured momentum. When something outside the robot pushes on it, a person, a wall, a heavier part, the gap grows, and its size is an estimate of that outside torque.

The nice properties are practical ones. It needs no acceleration measurement, so it avoids differentiating a noisy encoder. It has one tuning knob, a gain that trades speed for noise. And it runs in a few lines inside a 1 kHz loop.

The catch is that it trusts its inputs. If the encoder freezes, the observer sees momentum vanish and reports a huge outside torque that never happened. That is why, in my fault-isolation paper, the observer is only believed while a second residual, built from the motor’s back-EMF, says the encoder is healthy. One signal watches the world, the other watches the sensor.

25 Nov 2026ResearchIs it the sensor or the world? Isolating faults in a robot joint2 MIN

A robot joint that stops on every anomaly is safe but useless, and one that ignores anomalies is dangerous. The right reaction depends on what went wrong. If the encoder froze, the joint should go to a safe state. If it hit something, it should back off. If someone added a payload, it should adapt. A following-error check cannot tell these apart.

My paper draft uses two signals the FOC loop already computes. A back-EMF voltage residual reacts to encoder faults but is blind to load torque. A momentum observer reacts to external torque, and I only trust it while the voltage residual says the encoder is healthy. A short voltage-pulse test then separates collisions, payload changes and phase faults.

In simulation, with up to 30% model error, it isolated 299 of 300 injected faults, caught encoder faults within 0.51 ms, raised no false alarms in an hour of normal motion, and detected collisions about three times faster than a following-error check. The one miss was a slow encoder freeze labelled as a collision; the reaction was still safe, but the label was wrong. Hardware results come next, on the FOC testbed.

24 Nov 2026ResearchCan a cheap camera read a solar row's tilt in a haboob?2 MIN

Our capstone sponsor, ARRAY Technologies, needs to know when a follower row of a solar tracker drifts out of line with the drive row, without powering the follower. Alongside the team's selected infrared concept, I studied an alternative: a small camera on the drive row watching two coloured markers on the follower row.

Colour subtraction isolates the markers from sand, steel and sky, and a one-time teaching sweep against a reference inclinometer maps marker positions to angle. Two markers on opposite edges cancel wind sway on the camera post, and the colours give the tilt direction.

I built a physics-based field simulation and ran it through clear noon, sunrise into the lens, rain, hail, snow, fog, a haboob, 50 °C air, night with retroreflective markers, and ten-year-old sheeting. Every condition stayed inside the ±1° target. The useful part was what broke along the way: the torque tube hides the far marker between about +5° and +14°, which is why the firmware needs a near-marker fallback. Simulation found that before any hardware did.

11 Nov 2026ResearchCan a driving planner explain itself, and be telling the truth?2 MIN

Reasoning driving models now plan a path and explain it: slowing for a pedestrian, moving over for a bus. I wanted to know what it takes for those explanations to be true, so I built ExplainDrive, a small reason, plan and explain stack in pure Python with a closed-loop simulator and six hard scenarios.

The reasoner outputs constraints and the reasons for them; a Frenet sampling planner drives inside them. On a bus drifting over the centre line, a reactive baseline collided in every seeded run and the reasoning planner never did. Running the full reasoner only when the scene is complex cut its calls by 63% with no collisions, and the stack stayed collision-free up to 500 ms of end-to-end latency.

The faithfulness test re-runs each decision with one agent deleted: cited agents must matter, uncited ones must not. It reached 100% on both, but only after it caught a real bug, an explanation that blamed an oncoming car when the decision was actually driven by limited visibility. That is the part I would want any production system to have.

18 Oct 2026ResearchWhat should a delivery robot do when its sensors lie?2 MIN

Sidewalk delivery robots trust GPS, wheel odometry and other sensors to know where they are, and any of them can be spoofed. My second paper in progress, aimed at IEEE ARSO 2027, studies a localization monitor that notices when one sensor is lying and says so in plain language.

It runs a bank of leave-one-out extended Kalman filters, each ignoring one sensor, with a calibrated CUSUM detector watching for drift. When the filters disagree, consistency checks isolate the suspect sensor, with a threat-model tie-break for ambiguous cases, and an alert generator explains the decision to a human operator.

I evaluated it with 200 Monte Carlo trials per scenario across six attack types against four baseline methods, plus an ablation to see which piece earns its keep. It is simulation only, and I wrote it without a lab or collaborator, which made me extra careful to verify every claim against the raw results.

11 Jul 2026EmbeddedCan a bus schedule itself? Notes from SDCF2 MIN

SDCF asks whether microcontrollers on a shared RS-485 bus can organize into a collision-free TDMA schedule with no master node. In simulation, 63 nodes converged within 88 frames in the worst case.

The result I like best is the failure story. When a node drops, a peer takes over its slot in 3 frames against an analytical bound of 4. Deterministic means you can prove a number like that instead of hoping. The manuscript is close to done and the MPLAB X firmware is the next step.

16 May 2026EmbeddedThree nodes, one UART chain2 MIN

On the Amphibot our subsystems talked over a three-node UART daisy chain at 9600 baud, 8N1, with custom 12-byte ASCII frames. It looks old-fashioned and it is wonderful to debug: you can watch the whole bus in a terminal, or on a logic analyzer, and read what happened.

28 Mar 2026EmbeddedWhen the hardware peripheral fights you2 MIN

On the Amphibot sensor and HMI board, the PIC18F57Q83's hardware MSSP peripheral collided with another function we needed. Re-spinning the board was not an option, so I wrote a bit-banged I2C driver in C for the IMU, the OLED and the temperature and humidity sensor.

19 Apr 2025EmbeddedChebyshev or Butterworth? Pick by what you can lose2 MIN

Butterworth is maximally flat in the passband. Chebyshev gives a steeper roll-off in exchange for ripple. In MATLAB the comparison takes minutes, which makes it tempting to pick by looking at the plot.

A better question is what you can afford to give up. If the passband must stay honest, take flatness. If you need to reject a nearby interferer and can live with ripple, take steepness. Then check phase and group delay before you commit.

8 Feb 2024IndustrialGetting a vision system to agree with a conveyor2 MIN

A camera inspecting parts on a moving conveyor is a timing problem wearing a lens. The image is only as good as the instant you trigger it.

Trigger from a position or a sensor edge instead of a timer, keep the lighting boringly constant, and log every pass and fail with its image. When a station disagrees with a human, the saved picture settles it in seconds.

21 Nov 2023IndustrialLadder, structured text or function block?2 MIN

I use all three and choose by who will read it next. Ladder for interlocks and anything a technician must follow at 3 a.m.; structured text for math, arrays and state machines; function blocks to wrap a device once and reuse it.

If the logic has a clear power-flow story, draw it as ladder. If you are reaching for loops, write text. Either way, name things for the machine, not for yourself.

2 Sep 2023IndustrialReading ANSI/RIA 15.06 like a builder2 MIN

The standard reads like law until you build a guarded cell. Then it becomes practical questions: what stops the robot, how do you know it stopped, and what happens if the thing that stops it fails?

Light curtains, E-stops, dual-channel safety relays and interlocked guards are the answers. Dual channel matters most: two independent paths that must agree, so a single fault cannot silently defeat the stop. I now sketch the safety circuit before I write a line of motion code.

14 Jun 2023IndustrialWhat a robot cell teaches you that simulation cannot2 MIN

Offline programming gives you a path that works in a clean world. A real cell introduces what the simulator politely leaves out: a fixture two millimetres off, a cable that fights the wrist, a part that arrives rotated.

My habit since: jog slowly by hand first, teach the awkward points on the real hardware, and only then run at speed. Simulation is a plan. The cell is the truth.
